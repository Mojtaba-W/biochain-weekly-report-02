Five Designs.
One Lesson from Nature.

From buildings and everyday products to composites, textiles and surfaces, explore five ways biological strategies can inform design—and why performance testing matters.

MONDAY · ARCHITECTURE · BMS-11

Termite Mounds

Interconnected chambers inspire questions about airflow in high-rise buildings.

The lesson is to organize space for function, then test ventilation performance rather than assuming that a nature-inspired form will work.

Open original post ↗
Design prompt: what airflow, temperature or energy-use measure would you collect to compare a biomimetic ventilation concept with a conventional design?
💡
TUESDAY · PRODUCT DESIGN · BMS-12

Beyond Appearance

Use form, structure, materials, function and sustainability as distinct design lenses.

A product does not become bioinspired just because it looks natural. The design should adapt a biological strategy to a defined need and be evaluated against that need.

Open original post ↗
Design prompt: which product feature are you trying to improve—weight, durability, resource use, repairability or user experience—and how would you measure it?
🦴
WEDNESDAY · STRUCTURAL DESIGN · BMS-13

Bone Osteons

Hierarchical organization inspires composite architectures and crack-control strategies.

Bone’s multiscale organization offers a way to think about how material architecture can influence crack propagation and mechanical performance.

Open original post ↗
Design prompt: what would you measure first in a bioinspired composite—crack growth, toughness, strength, fatigue behaviour or mass?
🧵
THURSDAY · TEXTILE DESIGN · BMS-14

Animal Hide

Porous, multiscale fibres inspire thermal-insulating textiles and possible heat storage.

Combining fibrous structures with phase-change materials may add heat-storage functions, but the full textile must be tested under realistic conditions.

Open original post ↗
Design prompt: how would you test insulation, breathability, durability and thermal storage across repeated heating-and-cooling cycles?
🍃
FRIDAY · INDUSTRIAL DESIGN · BMS-15

Lotus Leaf

Self-cleaning surfaces depend on more than water repellency alone.

Surface texture, flexibility, internal structure, geometry and the petiole may work together. The design claim should match the evidence and conditions tested.

Open original post ↗
Design prompt: what counts as self-cleaning in the intended use—less retained dirt, fewer cleaning cycles, lower water use, or a measurable change in surface performance?

Nature is not a catalogue of shapes to copy. It is a source of functional strategies to understand, adapt and evaluate. A bioinspired design is not automatically sustainable or ready for commercial use: evidence matters.
